## The Investigation

A colleague asks you a question over coffee:

> "Does the Frankfurt market behave differently after rainy days? I think DAX closes weaker when traders are stuck inside."

You have three data sources you've heard about: web logs, stock prices, and weather. You don't quite remember the index names. You have an afternoon to find out.

Along the way you will hit every classic search performance trap from the rulebook — and learn how to avoid them.

---

### Act 1 — Where is my data?

You start by listing what's available. Your first instinct is to look at hosts across the whole environment:

<SplunkSearch earliest="-24h" latest="now" spl={`| metadata type=hosts index=*`}></SplunkSearch>

This works, but it pulls from every index — including internal ones. Open Job Inspector and note the bucket scan count.

A faster way: `tstats` reads the same information from the tsidx files directly, no event scanning required.

<SplunkSearch earliest="-24h" latest="now" spl={`| tstats count WHERE index=* OR index=_* BY index
| sort -count`}></SplunkSearch>

You now see `s4c_www`, `s4c_stock_indices`, `s4c_meteo_historic`, and `s4c_weather`. The first is web logs (not relevant for this investigation). The other three are your investigation surface.

> **Lesson:** `tstats` on the tsidx is the fastest way to get index-level metadata.

---

### Act 2 — Get to know the events

Before you write any aggregation, look at the shape of the data.

<SplunkSearch earliest="-7d" latest="now" spl={`index=s4c_stock_indices
| head 5`}></SplunkSearch>

Take note of the fields: `index_name`, `exchange_city`, `symbol`, `open`, `close`, `volume`.

Same for weather:

<SplunkSearch earliest="-7d" latest="now" spl={`index=s4c_meteo_historic
| head 5`}></SplunkSearch>

Note: `city`, `temperature_2m_max`, `precipitation_sum`, `date`.

The shared key will be `exchange_city` (stocks) matched with `city` (weather), aligned by date. Good — no `join` will be required.

> **Lesson:** sample first, aggregate later. Knowing field names up front saves dead-end queries.

---

### Act 3 — One city, one question — but filter late

You want DAX's average close over the last year. Your first attempt filters too late:

<SplunkSearch earliest="-1y" latest="now" spl={`index=s4c_stock_indices
| stats avg(close) as avg_close by index_name
| where index_name="DAX"`}></SplunkSearch>

Splunk computes the average for **all 9 indexes** and then throws 8 away. Better:

<SplunkSearch earliest="-1y" latest="now" spl={`index=s4c_stock_indices index_name=DAX
| stats avg(close) as avg_close`}></SplunkSearch>

Run both. In Job Inspector, compare `eventCount` and `command.search.index` time.

> **Lesson:** push every filter as far left as possible. Index filters and field filters belong in the base search, not after a pipe.

---

### Act 4 — Weather summary, same rule

Frankfurt's average daily max temperature. The natural-but-wrong version filters with `| search` after a pipe:

<SplunkSearch earliest="-1y" latest="now" spl={`index=s4c_meteo_historic
| stats avg(temperature_2m_max) by city
| search city=Frankfurt`}></SplunkSearch>

The indexers already did the work for every city. Fix it by filtering in the base search:

<SplunkSearch earliest="-1y" latest="now" spl={`index=s4c_meteo_historic city=Frankfurt
| stats avg(temperature_2m_max) as avg_temp`}></SplunkSearch>

> **Lesson:** `| search` after the first pipe almost always indicates a filter that belongs in the base search.

---

### Act 5 — When you just need counts, use `tstats`

How many trading days do you have per index?

A regular search scans every event:

<SplunkSearch earliest="-10y" latest="now" spl={`index=s4c_stock_indices
| stats count by index_name`}></SplunkSearch>

`tstats` reads the same count from the tsidx files — no raw events touched:

<SplunkSearch earliest="-10y" latest="now" spl={`| tstats count WHERE index=s4c_stock_indices BY index_name
| sort -count`}></SplunkSearch>

Both return the same numbers. Compare `runDuration` in Job Inspector — usually 10–50x faster.

> **Lesson:** `tstats` reads from the tsidx and only sees indexed fields. For aggregations on `index`, `sourcetype`, `host`, `source`, and indexed field extractions, it is dramatically faster.

---

### Act 6 — Two datasets, no `join`

The natural reach for combining datasets is `join`. It usually hurts. Try it first:

<SplunkSearch earliest="-1y" latest="now" spl={`index=s4c_stock_indices index_name=DAX
| eval day=strftime(_time, "%Y-%m-%d"), close=tonumber(mvindex(close,0))
| stats avg(close) as avg_close by day
| join day [ search index=s4c_meteo_historic city=Frankfurt
    | eval day=coalesce(mvindex(date,0), strftime(_time, "%Y-%m-%d"))
    | stats avg(temperature_2m_max) as avg_temp by day ]`}></SplunkSearch>

`join` runs the subsearch sequentially, caps the inner result at 50k rows by default, and has its own time limit. The scalable pattern is `append` followed by a collapsing `stats`:

<SplunkSearch earliest="-1y" latest="now" spl={`index=s4c_stock_indices index_name=DAX
| eval day=strftime(_time, "%Y-%m-%d"), close=tonumber(mvindex(close,0))
| stats avg(close) as avg_close by day
| append [ search index=s4c_meteo_historic city=Frankfurt
    | eval day=coalesce(mvindex(date,0), strftime(_time, "%Y-%m-%d"))
    | stats avg(temperature_2m_max) as avg_temp by day ]
| stats values(avg_close) as avg_close values(avg_temp) as avg_temp by day
| where isnotnull(avg_close) AND isnotnull(avg_temp)`}></SplunkSearch>

> **Lesson:** `append` runs the second branch in parallel. The collapsing `stats values(...) by day` merges the two streams. No subsearch limit, no sequential wait.

---

### Act 7 — The payoff: today's market, yesterday's weather

The original question wasn't about same-day correlation — it was about whether **yesterday's** weather predicts today's market. That is a lag feature. `streamstats` builds it event-by-event without leaving the streaming pipeline.

<SplunkSearch earliest="-2y" latest="now" spl={`index=s4c_stock_indices index_name=DAX
| eval day=strftime(_time, "%Y-%m-%d"), o=tonumber(mvindex(open,0)), c=tonumber(mvindex(close,0)), day_return=((c-o)/o)*100
| stats avg(day_return) as day_return by day
| append [ search index=s4c_meteo_historic city=Frankfurt
    | eval day=coalesce(mvindex(date,0), strftime(_time, "%Y-%m-%d")), rain=tonumber(precipitation_sum)
    | stats avg(rain) as rain_mm by day ]
| stats values(day_return) as day_return values(rain_mm) as rain_mm by day
| where isnotnull(day_return) AND isnotnull(rain_mm)
| sort 0 day
| streamstats current=f window=1 last(rain_mm) as yesterday_rain
| where isnotnull(yesterday_rain)
| eval weather_yesterday=case(yesterday_rain>=5, "Rainy (5mm+)", true(), "Dry (<5mm)")
| stats count as trading_days avg(day_return) as avg_return_pct stdev(day_return) as stdev_return by weather_yesterday
| eval avg_return_pct=round(avg_return_pct,3), stdev_return=round(stdev_return,3)`}></SplunkSearch>

You have your first answer. Read it carefully:

- `trading_days` — how many observations per scenario. Bigger samples are more trustworthy.
- `avg_return_pct` — the mean daily return after rainy vs dry yesterdays.
- `stdev_return` — variability. A small mean difference with high variance is not a signal.

> **Lesson:** `streamstats current=f window=1 last(x)` is the idiomatic Splunk lag-feature pattern. It stays in the streaming pipeline, so it scales.

### Where to go next

The skeleton you just built generalises. Try these variations:

- Swap `index_name=DAX` and `city=Frankfurt` for `index_name="S&P 500"` and `city="New York"` — does the same effect appear in a different market?
- Replace the rainfall threshold with a temperature bucket: `case(yesterday_temp>=25,"Hot", yesterday_temp>=10,"Mild", true(),"Cold")`.
- Use a 3-day rolling window instead of yesterday only: `streamstats current=f window=3 avg(rain_mm) as rain_3d`.
- Add `index_name` to the final `by` to compare all 9 markets in one search.

After each variation, open Job Inspector. Did your change make the search faster or slower? Why?

Everything in the rulebook applies here. You now have a method.
